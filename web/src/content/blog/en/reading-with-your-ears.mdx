Some people read worse than they understand. Put like that it sounds odd, but it is one of the most ordinary situations in the world: the letter-decoding machine runs slowly, gets tired or wanders off, while the head behind it works perfectly well. What fails is not comprehension. It is the road the text comes in by.

Listening changes the road. It is not magic and it fixes nothing on its own. But for a lot of people it turns an impossible text into a possible one, and that is already quite a lot.

This article is practical: which settings matter, what to expect and what not to, and one technical distinction that gets confused constantly.

## A screen reader and a content reader are not the same thing

This is the most common confusion and it is worth clearing up first.

A **screen reader** — VoiceOver on iPhone and Mac, NVDA or Narrator on Windows, TalkBack on Android — narrates the entire interface: buttons, menus, the state of a checkbox, where the focus is. It exists so that someone can operate a device without seeing the screen, and it is a mature, free and very powerful assistive technology. If that is your situation, nothing replaces a screen reader.

A **content reader** does something far more modest: it takes one specific text — this article, a PDF, a chapter — and says it out loud, with its controls in plain sight. It exists for someone who sees the screen perfectly well but prefers, or needs, to listen.

The two coexist without fighting. And a serious reading app has to get along with the other one: correct labels, sensible focus order, respect for the system text size and for the reduce-motion setting.

## Dyslexia: the cost of decoding

Decoding letters consumes resources. When decoding is expensive, less attention is left for what the text says; that is why an ordinary text can be exhausting even when its content is easy. It is the usual problem: all the energy is spent at the door and none is left for the house.

Listening removes that toll. And listening **while seeing the text** — bimodal reading, it is called — is what most people describe as useful: the ear carries the rhythm, the eyes follow the words, and the pairing between what is seen and what is heard happens by itself.

That is where on-screen following really matters, and where most automatic readers fall down: they highlight a whole paragraph, or estimate by eye which word is due. In Yappy the engine publishes the exact second each sentence starts and ends, and the highlight advances on that real mark. The difference between an exact highlight and an approximate one is the difference between a help and a hindrance: if the highlight lags half a second, all it does is distract.

## ADHD: the problem is not attention, it is starting

A lot of people with ADHD describe the same thing: reading is not hard, starting to read is. And sitting still in a chair for forty minutes is, too.

Listening solves both sideways. Reading stops demanding stillness — you can walk, wash up, draw, squeeze something in your hands — and starting costs one tap instead of an internal negotiation. Some people find 1.5x helps precisely because there is less gap between words to slip away into; others find the opposite. There is no single recommendation here, and I would be suspicious of anyone who offers one.

What can be said safely is this: when reading no longer depends on sitting down, the number of available moments in a week multiplies. And what produces more reading is not reading better: it is reading more often.

## Tired eyes, migraines, and everyone else

There are many reasons not to want to look at a screen any more: presbyopia, dry eyes, a migraine on the way, surgery, fourteen hours in front of a monitor, a Monday. Listening does not ask your eyes for permission.

One small detail that matters a lot here: Yappy's reader works with the screen off and the phone in your pocket, with controls on the lock screen and on your headphones. Listening to a chapter without taking the phone out is, for a fair number of people, the difference between reading and not reading.

## The settings that actually help

In order of importance, according to the people who use it daily:

1. **Speed, going up.** Start at 1x and climb a little over a few days. Everyone ends up listening faster than they started, and nobody can stand starting fast. Doing it the other way round — setting 2x on day one and giving up — is the most common mistake.
2. **The voice, properly auditioned.** Half an hour with a voice that irritates you is half an hour lost. There are [ten voices](/en/voices/) and it is worth hearing four or five before deciding. The one you like in five seconds is not always the one you can stand for thirty minutes.
3. **On-screen following.** For reading with your eyes at the same time. It can be turned off if it distracts, which it does for some people.
4. **Text size and contrast.** The app follows the system text size. If a large size helps you, set it in Settings and everything else inherits it.
5. **Pause by tapping anywhere.** It sounds like a design detail and it is pure accessibility: when you have to hit a twenty-pixel button with wet hands, the function does not exist. In the reader, the whole screen is the button.
6. **Skip by sentence, not by fifteen seconds.** Going back "a bit" almost always means going back to the start of the sentence. Fifteen-second jumps land in the middle of a word.

There are more, listed one by one, on [the accessibility page](/en/accessibility/).

## What this is not

It is worth being very clear here, because too much gets promised in this area:

- **It is not a medical device.** It does not treat, diagnose or improve any condition. It reads text aloud.
- **It does not replace a screen reader** for someone who cannot see the screen.
- **It does not replace an educational accommodation**, or the judgement of whoever is supporting a student.
- **It does not guarantee comprehension.** Listening to a dense text at double speed is not reading it; it is an elegant way of missing the point. For studying, what works is combining: one pass with your ears and another with your eyes, or listening with the text in front of you.

## How to start without spending anything

Take a text you already want to read — not a test one: a real one — share it with the app and listen for five minutes with the text in front of you. If you are still there after five minutes, it works for you. If not, no harm done: some people do not get on with listening, and it is useful to find that out early.

And if it does work, the change you will see is not that you read better. It is that you will read in the moments that did not use to count as reading time.
